## 01 — The 10,000-item problem

Look at the right panel. Three numbers: items, height per item, total height. **10,000 items &times; 36px = 360,000 pixels** of content. That's roughly 900 screen-heights of scrollable space packed into a single `<div>`.

Now look at the tiny accent bar at the top of the red block. That's your viewport — 380 pixels. You can see about 11 items at a time. The other 9,989 are invisible. They're below the fold, above the fold, or scrolled past entirely. No human will ever see more than ~11 items simultaneously.

But here's the question that drives everything in this module: **if those 9,989 items are invisible, why do they exist in the DOM?**

The answer, usually, is inertia. The obvious implementation — `items.map(item => <div>{item}</div>)` — creates a DOM node for every item because that's what `.map()` does. Each node enters the layout tree, gets styled, participates in paint, and sits in memory. At 100 items, this is invisible. At 1,000, it's a slight hiccup. At 10,000, it's a problem.

Drag the slider and watch the total height recalculate. At 100K items, you're asking the browser to manage a `<div>` taller than a skyscraper — 3.6 million pixels — and you can still only see 11 items.

The next step shows you what that actually costs.

## 02 — Feel the DOM weight

The demo just switched to an actual scrollable list. Every item you see is a real DOM node — 2,000 of them (capped from 10,000 to prevent your browser from locking up). Look at the red badge in the header: **2,000 DOM nodes**.

Scroll it. On a fast machine, it might feel fine. On a phone, or a 2019 laptop, or in a tab with twenty other tabs open, it won't. The problem isn't the scrolling itself — browsers are excellent at scrolling. The problem is that every DOM node:

1. **Occupies memory.** Each node is a C++ object in the browser's rendering engine. At 36px &times; 2,000 items, the DOM tree holds references to 2,000 layout boxes, 2,000 paint records, 2,000 sets of computed styles.

2. **Participates in layout.** When you resize the window, or a sibling changes height, the browser recalculates positions for every node in the flow. With 2,000 absolutely-positioned nodes, the browser traverses 2,000 elements during each layout pass.

3. **Competes for the frame budget.** At 60fps you have 16.6ms per frame. Layout alone for 2,000 nodes can eat 4-8ms of that budget, leaving almost no room for JavaScript, paint, and compositing.

Now imagine this is a product catalog with images, prices, and interactive buttons — not plain text rows. Each item becomes 5-10 DOM nodes. A 2,000-item list becomes 10,000-20,000 DOM nodes. That's where browsers start visibly dropping frames.

The state inspector shows `domNodes: 2000`. In the next step, we'll see why most of them are wasted work.

</section>

## 03 — Your viewport is a keyhole

A minimap just appeared on the left. Look at it closely — the gold bar is your viewport. The rest of the track represents the total list height. Your viewport is a sliver. A keyhole. You're looking at 11 items through a window that covers **0.1%** of the total scroll height.

Scroll the list and watch the minimap. The gold viewport indicator moves, but it barely budges relative to the total height. Meanwhile, every scroll event processes all 2,000 DOM nodes.

Now look at the scroll area. The items inside the viewport are highlighted with a gold left border. Everything else is dimmed. That dimming represents **wasted work** — DOM nodes that exist, take memory, participate in layout, but are completely invisible to the user.

The state inspector now shows `viewportStart` and `viewportEnd` — the index range of items actually visible. It also shows `wastedNodes` — the number of DOM nodes that exist for no visible purpose. Scroll to the middle of the list: `viewportStart: 500`, `viewportEnd: 510`, `wastedNodes: 1989`. Nearly two thousand nodes doing nothing.

```tsx
// What the browser processes every frame:
for (const node of allNodes) { // 2,000 iterations
  computeStyle(node);
  computeLayout(node);
  paintIfVisible(node); // only ~11 actually paint
}
```

This is the fundamental insight: **the browser doesn't know which items are visible until after it processes all of them**. It dutifully lays out every node, then clips to the viewport and throws away 99.5% of the work.

What if we could tell the browser in advance which items to skip?

## 04 — The flip

A toggle just appeared in the toolbar: **Windowing**. Click it.

Watch three things happen simultaneously:

1. The DOM badge flips from red to green. The number drops from 2,000 to roughly **17**. The list looks and scrolls identically, but 99.2% of the DOM nodes just vanished.

2. The minimap's rendered band (the green region) shrinks from the full track to a tiny sliver — only the items near the viewport are rendered. Everything else is empty space.

3. The comparison banner at the bottom shows the ratio. Windowed: 17 nodes. Naive: 2,000 nodes. That's **~120&times; fewer DOM nodes** for the same visual result.

Toggle it off. Back to 2,000. Toggle it on. Back to 17. Same list. Same scrollbar. Same items. Dramatically different cost.

How? The trick has three parts:

**1. A spacer element.** A single empty `<div>` with `height: 360,000px`. This creates the native scrollbar — the browser sees a tall container and renders the correct scroll thumb. No items needed.

**2. Positioned items.** Only the ~17 items near the viewport actually exist in the DOM. Each one is positioned absolutely inside the spacer using `transform: translateY()` at its calculated pixel offset.

**3. A scroll handler.** On every scroll event, we calculate which items should be visible (`Math.floor(scrollTop / itemHeight)`) and mount only those. Items that scroll out of view are unmounted — removed from the DOM entirely.

```tsx
const startIndex = Math.floor(scrollTop / ITEM_HEIGHT);
const endIndex = startIndex + Math.ceil(viewportHeight / ITEM_HEIGHT);
// Only render items[startIndex..endIndex]
```

The result is a contract with the browser: "We'll handle visibility. You just render these 17 nodes." The browser's job shrinks from a 2,000-node layout to a 17-node layout — every single frame.

Look at `spacerHeight: 360,000px` in the state inspector. That single number is the entire illusion. The scrollbar thinks there are 10,000 items because the container is 360,000px tall. There are actually 17.

## 05 — The scroll-to-render pipeline

Scroll the list and watch the pipeline visualization that just appeared below the scroller. Four boxes, connected by arrows, updating in real time:

**scrollTop** &rarr; **startIdx** &rarr; **mount** &rarr; **DOM**

This is the complete computation that runs on every scroll event. Let's trace it:

1. **scrollTop** — the raw pixel offset from the browser's scroll event. Scroll down a bit and watch this number climb.

2. **startIdx** — `Math.floor(scrollTop / 36)`. This is the key insight of fixed-height windowing: because every item is exactly 36px tall, we can convert a pixel offset to an item index with a single division. No measurement needed. No DOM queries. Pure math.

3. **mount** — the range of items to render: `[startIdx, startIdx + visibleCount]`. These are the only items that will exist in the DOM.

4. **DOM** — the actual number of mounted nodes. This stays constant regardless of scroll position — whether you're at the top, middle, or bottom of a 100,000-item list, the browser handles the same ~17 nodes.

```tsx
function onScroll(e) {
  const scrollTop = e.currentTarget.scrollTop;
  const start = Math.floor(scrollTop / ITEM_HEIGHT);
  const end = Math.min(totalItems - 1, start + visibleCount - 1);
  // React re-renders with items[start..end]
}
```

The `Math.floor` division is why this works at 60fps. It's O(1) — constant time regardless of list size. A million-item list computes the same division as a hundred-item list. No binary search, no DOM measurement, no layout query. One division, one comparison, one render.

Now look at each row's label in the list: `translateY(18000px)`, `translateY(18036px)`, etc. Each item is positioned exactly where it should appear in the scroll space. The browser doesn't need to know about the 9,983 items that don't exist — it just paints these 17 items at their calculated offsets.

**Why transforms, not `top/left`:** Setting `top: 18000px` triggers layout — the browser recalculates positions for the element and potentially its siblings. `transform: translateY(18000px)` is compositor-only — the GPU moves an already-painted layer without touching layout or paint. This is the difference between 0.1ms and 4ms per frame.

## 06 — The overscan buffer

An overscan slider just appeared above the scroller. Drag it to **0** and then scroll fast — flick the scroll wheel hard. See the flash? For a fraction of a second, the edges of the viewport go blank before new items appear. That's because the browser scrolled faster than React could mount the next batch of items.

The timeline looks like this:

1. Browser scrolls 200px in one frame (fast flick)
2. React receives the scroll event
3. React calculates the new visible range
4. React renders the new items
5. Browser paints — but between steps 1 and 4, the viewport was empty

Now drag the overscan slider to **3**. Scroll fast again. The flash is gone. Check the state inspector: `overscan: ±3` means we render 3 extra items above and below the viewport. When the browser scrolls faster than React can update, those buffer items are already in the DOM, already painted, already waiting.

The tradeoff is direct: at `overscan: 3`, we mount 17 + 6 = 23 items instead of 17. That's 35% more DOM nodes, but still 99.8% fewer than naive rendering. The cost is negligible. The benefit — no blank flashes — is immediately visible.

```tsx
const renderStart = Math.max(0, viewportStart - overscan);
const renderEnd = Math.min(totalItems - 1, viewportEnd + overscan);
```

Most virtualisation libraries default to 3-5 items of overscan. Too little and fast scrolling reveals blanks. Too much and you're mounting items that defeat the purpose. The sweet spot depends on your scroll behavior: trackpad users generate small, frequent scroll deltas (low overscan is fine). Mouse wheel users generate large, infrequent jumps (higher overscan helps). Programmatic `scrollTo()` calls jump instantly — you'd want to pre-mount the entire target range.

Look at the row labels now. Items inside the viewport show `translateY(...)`. Items in the overscan buffer show `translateY(...) (buffer)` with a blue left border. They exist in the DOM — they just haven't scrolled into view yet. When they do, there's zero delay because they were already rendered.

This is the complete virtual scroller: a spacer for the scrollbar, positioned items for the viewport, a scroll handler for the math, and an overscan buffer for smooth edges. Four concepts, ~50 lines of core logic, capable of handling hundreds of thousands of items at 60fps.
